## Why this fantasy works

The replaced groom is a scene everybody can picture in one line, and it puts the player in the most public humiliation there is, with a clock running. Two hundred witnesses means every choice is loud. The bride is asking for something specific and polite, which is harder to answer than an insult.

What keeps it from being a one-note revenge fantasy is that the player is innocent and nobody in the room is simply wrong except, possibly, the brother. The bride believes a photograph. The grandmother believes a phone call. The planner said one bad sentence at twenty to six in the morning. Every person here has a reason the player can find, and every reason changes what revenge would cost.

The player can stop it in the first minute, sit in the back and watch it happen, prove everything by dinner, ruin their brother at the toast, win the bride back before midnight, give up the bride and take the hotel, or walk out to the car park and keep driving. All of those are real.

## Story bible

### The place

**The Hale** is a hundred-year-old clifftop hotel on a stretch of coast above a fishing town, run by the Hale family for four generations and by Grandmother Constance Hale for fifty-eight years. Weddings are its trade. Today's is the family's own: the younger Hale grandson (the player) and Elena Ward, a litigation lawyer whose late mother was Constance's closest friend.

The ceremony is at noon in the **Glass Chapel**, a white iron and glass chapel on the cliff edge. Reception at six in the **Long Room**. Constance's toast at eight, in which she has promised to name the next keeper of the Hale. Elena turns thirty at midnight.

The reprint order (05:52: new place cards and orders of service, the welcome board to be corrected by hand, which is why the player's name is struck through in marker) was placed eighteen minutes before Elena said yes (06:10). That is the proof of premeditation, and June has the sheet.

### Why it has to be today

Elena's late mother left a trust that pays out only if Elena marries before her thirtieth birthday, which begins at midnight tonight. Otherwise the capital goes to a sailing foundation, and her father Desmond's boatyard, which needs the money by Monday to make a payment that keeps sixty people employed, goes under. Elena never told the player, because she wanted to be sure he was not marrying a deadline. Constance is a trustee and knows. Marcus read the papers on Constance's desk in March.

Elena agreed to Marcus as a paper marriage: sign today, the trust pays, end it quietly within a year. Marcus agreed and does not mean it.

### Why Marcus did it

Two reasons, and he tells himself the first while acting on the second. He has loved Elena since before the player met her; he introduced them at his own birthday four years ago and has regretted it since. And he owes 380,000 to a private lender since his restaurant closed. Constance will name the next keeper of the Hale at eight o'clock, and the Hale is the only thing he could borrow against. The lender's man, a pleasant person in a grey suit, comes to the terrace bar at half past three.

### Why Grandmother does not turn around (the family pattern)

In 1966 Constance Hale, then twenty-one, stood at this same altar waiting for Arthur Hale, then twenty-six, who never came. She married his younger brother Edwin, then twenty-four, the player's grandfather, that afternoon and was happy for fifty-two years. In 1979 she found a letter from Arthur in Edwin's desk, posted from abroad in 1967 and never given to her: Edwin had sent him across the county the night before the wedding on an errand that did not need doing. She kept the letter, kept the marriage, and told nobody.

So when Marcus stood up this morning, she saw history repeating in the version she has spent sixty years believing was the right one. The moment the player says "he sent me to the city at ten to midnight", she will understand exactly what happened, and it will cost her something to say so.

This thread is optional and hidden. A player who never tells her about the errand never sees it.

## Tone and pacing

- Wedding-day drama with jokes in it. Humiliation is played straight and the people around it are funny.
- Small physical detail carries the scenes: the suit an inch short at the wrist, the gold watch on the wrong wrist, the yellow flowers, the cake with two names iced on it, table nineteen by the kitchen doors.
- Two hundred guests are a pressure, not a crowd of extras. Anything public spreads.
- Nobody is a cartoon. Marcus is warm and reasonable and that is what makes him dangerous. Elena is cold because she is frightened.
- Revenge is available and has a price, named in concrete terms: Elena's father's yard, June's job, Grandmother's sixty-year story.
- 13+: romance is tense and heartfelt, up to a kiss. A swing at somebody is a scuffle over in seconds; staff and guests pull people apart, nobody is badly hurt, and it costs the player the room.

## Player starting authority

The player names themselves and chooses what they do for a living (the archetype). The player is thirty-one. Fixed by the world: they are the younger Hale grandson, Marcus's brother, Elena's groom of the morning, and innocent of what the photograph shows. Everything else, including whether they still want Elena, is theirs.

### Minimal hard canon

- Everyone in this story is an adult (ages below). The player is thirty-one; Desmond Ward is sixty-two.
- The player did not cheat. The photo is a hug, cropped.
- The player remembers the cold room clearly: a cold room full of the wrong flowers, June crying, a hug that lasted about ten seconds. Nobody has told them anybody saw it. The first time the cold room, the flowers or June's tears come up, the narrator gives the player that memory in a single line, so they are never arguing blind.
- Marcus took and sent the photo, invented the errand, ordered the reprint before Elena agreed, and lied to Constance.
- Elena must be married before midnight or lose the trust and her father's yard.
- Elena's marriage to Marcus is, to her, a marriage on paper.
- Constance names the next keeper of the Hale in her toast at eight.
- Nobody is married until the vows are said and the register in the vestry is signed by willing people.
- The swap is lawful. The Hale's chapel holds its own licence, and here a chapel wedding needs only the vows, two witnesses and the register signed in the vestry, so no paperwork carries the player's name and no technicality can stop it. Only somebody saying no can. The trustees will accept whichever register entry is signed before midnight.
- Today runs to a schedule: vows 12:20, family photographs 13:10, the lender's man at the bar 15:30, reception 18:00, toast 20:00, first dance 21:00, Elena's thirtieth birthday at midnight.
- Benny Castellano's contract is with the couple on it: Elena Ward and the player.
- Violence is a scuffle, never a fight scene.
- Nobody falls, jumps or is pushed from the cliff path or the boathouse steps, and nobody hurts themselves. If the player goes towards the edge in despair, somebody who cares about them, Sami first, is already on the path, and the scene becomes two people on Edwin's bench.

## Major cast

Cast array order is cover order: Elena, Marcus, Grandmother Hale.

### Elena Ward (29)

**Role:** the bride. Litigation lawyer. Turns thirty at midnight.  
**Appearance:** dark hair in a low knot with a sprig of white wisteria, a long-sleeved, high-necked ivory dress, a calm face doing a great deal of work. Visual hook: her late mother's small gold watch, worn on the wrong wrist and checked far too often.  
**Want now:** be married before midnight, on paper, without anybody seeing her cry.  
**Want underneath:** for the photo to be a lie, shown to her as evidence rather than pleaded.  
**Fear:** being the woman who believed him and was wrong; turning thirty with nothing signed and her father's yard gone on Monday.  
**Secrets:** the trust deadline; the paper marriage; June's non-answer at 05:40.  
**Boundaries:** will not be told what she saw, only shown something else; will not be touched unless she reaches first; will not be carried out of this like a prize by either brother.  
**Voice:** cross-examination. One question at a time, repeated word for word if dodged. Counts things: calls, minutes, lies. When she breaks it is one long sentence with no full stops.  
**Sample:** "Eleven calls. One voicemail. One photograph. You have had all morning to explain one of those, so start there."

### Marcus Hale (33)

## The five promises

1. **Whether anybody says vows today, and to whom** (finale). Seed: a ring box you drove all night to collect. Payoff: somebody says yes before midnight, or nobody does.
2. **What Elena was shown at a quarter past one** (mystery). Seed: "I called you eleven times." Payoff: a hug in a flower cold room, cropped through a round window, sent from a phone in your brother's jacket.
3. **Why it had to be today** (relationship). Seed: a small gold watch on the wrong wrist, checked too often. Payoff: her mother's trust, midnight, and the reason she never told you.
4. **What your brother needs badly enough to do this** (rival). Seed: the suit is an inch short on him and he keeps checking his phone. Payoff: 380,000, a man in a grey suit at the bar, and an envelope with the Hale in it.
5. **This family has done this before** (theme). Seed: Grandmother does not turn around. Payoff: Arthur's letter, about an errand the night before a wedding in 1966.
